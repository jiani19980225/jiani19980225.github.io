01Philosophy

What I believe.

Many of the most useful things I know, I learned from operations: every system has constraints, information is never complete, and almost nothing should be thrown away too quickly. These are the principles I try to bring to research, to the people I work with, and to the rest of my life. Like any good model, they are a work in progress.

02On research

How I approach research.

  1. 01

    Judge a model by the decision it changes.

    It is easy to fall in love with a clever method. I try to start from the decision someone actually has to make, and to measure success by whether that decision gets better. If nothing would change, the analysis isn’t finished.

  2. 02

    Say exactly what you know.

    Evidence earns trust only when it is reported honestly: what was tested, what wasn’t, and how sure we are. I would rather make a smaller claim that holds than a bigger one that doesn’t.

  3. 03

    Curiosity doesn’t need a title.

    My research grew out of questions from my day-to-day work that I couldn’t stop thinking about. You don’t need permission to be curious. You need a question you care about, good collaborators, and the patience to keep going.

03On people

How I work with others.

  1. 04

    Leave people better than you found them.

    Some of the work I value most will never appear in a paper: helping junior analysts grow into confident problem solvers. Teaching someone to frame a problem well outlasts any single analysis I could hand them.

  2. 05

    Make it useful to everyone at the table.

    A model that only its author understands rarely changes anything. I try to explain assumptions and limits in plain language, whether I’m talking with executives, engineers, or the people closest to the work.

04On life

What operations taught me about living.

  1. 06

    Everything deserves a second life.

    In reverse logistics, a returned device isn’t waste; it’s an asset whose next use hasn’t been decided yet. I try to treat setbacks the same way: inspect them honestly, keep what’s valuable, and route the rest toward something new.

  2. 07

    Steady beats spectacular.

    My forecasting research favors predictions that don’t swing wildly from week to week, because steadiness is what lets others plan. I try to be that kind of colleague and friend: consistent, reliable, and there when it counts.

  3. 08

    Decide with the information you have.

    Waiting for perfect information is itself a decision, and usually an expensive one. I try to act on the best evidence available, stay open to new signals, and adjust without regret.

Optimization taught me that the best solution respects its constraints. I try to live that way: aim high, work within what is real, and keep improving.
